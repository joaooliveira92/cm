# Onboarding in *the game*
The onboarding of is very different from the guided introductions found in modern sports-management games. There was no elaborate tutorial, assistant-led checklist, contextual overlay, or carefully staged first week. Instead, the game used a **simulation-first onboarding model**: the player configured the football world, created a manager, selected a club, and was then placed directly into the everyday work of football management.

It was an onboarding process that trusted the player to explore, make mistakes, consult the manual, and gradually understand the relationship between the game’s many screens.

---

## 1. The opening experience

After launching the game, the player arrived at a relatively functional main menu. The important choices were essentially:

- Start a new game
- Load an existing game
- Join or create a network game
- Change preferences
- Access other administrative options

The presentation was utilitarian rather than cinematic. There was no introductory video attempting to explain the manager’s role and no narrative sequence in which the chairman welcomed the player to the club.

This communicated an important idea immediately: **the database and simulation were the main attractions**.


---

## 2. Creating the football world

The first substantial onboarding stage happened before the player created a manager. The game asked the player to determine the size and scope of the simulation.

### Selecting nations and leagues

The player chose which countries and divisions would be active. This was more than a content-selection menu. It defined:

- Which clubs could be managed
- Which competitions would be simulated in detail
- How large the active player database would be
- How many transfer markets would operate
- How quickly the game would process each day
- How much memory and processing power the save would require

For example, a player could activate only England and begin with a relatively focused domestic game. Alternatively, the player might load England, Spain, Italy, Germany, France, Brazil, Argentina, Portugal, and several smaller nations, creating a much richer but slower football world.


### Full-detail simulation

The league-selection screen also exposed the player to the idea of simulation detail. A competition running in full detail received more comprehensive match processing than a less important background league.

This was sophisticated functionality, but the onboarding did not explain it through a progressive tutorial. Players had to understand that more detail produced a richer world at the cost of longer processing times.

### Why this stage mattered

This was effectively the game’s first major managerial decision, even though it occurred before the manager existed. Selecting leagues established the boundaries of the player’s career:

- A narrowly configured game was faster but offered fewer future jobs.
- A broadly configured game supported an international managerial career.
- Loading lower divisions made long-term, small-club careers possible.
- Selecting several nations created a more varied scouting and transfer environment.

Contemporary reviews emphasized that choosing between a wealthy elite club and a lower-league side produced substantially different experiences. Elite clubs offered money, famous players, and extreme expectations, while smaller clubs imposed limited budgets, weaker facilities, and the risk of losing promising players to richer teams.

---

## 3. Creating the manager

Once the database had been generated, the player created a managerial identity.

The profile normally included basic personal information such as:

- Name
- Date of birth
- Nationality
- The club to be managed

The manager was not constructed through a modern role-playing attribute system. There were no points to distribute among coaching, recruitment, motivation, or tactical knowledge. Likewise, the player did not choose a detailed professional history designed to influence starting attributes.

The identity was primarily representational. It allowed the player to insert themselves, or a fictional manager, into the football world.

A retrospective description of the process captures its abruptness well: the player entered a name, birth date, and nationality, chose a club from the selected leagues, and was then effectively “thrown in at the deep end” of management. citeturn1search8

This simplicity had two consequences:

1. **Very little friction:** a returning player could begin a career quickly.
2. **Very little preparation:** a newcomer received almost no explanation before assuming responsibility for an entire club.

---

## 4. Selecting a club

Club selection was the point at which onboarding became difficulty selection.

Although the game did not explicitly present “easy,” “normal,” and “hard” modes, the chosen club performed that function.

### Starting with an elite club

Selecting a club such as Manchester United, Arsenal, Real Madrid, Milan, or Juventus generally gave the player:

- A strong first-team squad
- High-quality coaches
- Better training facilities
- A larger transfer budget
- Greater wage capacity
- Access to famous players
- European competition

However, this also brought:

- High board expectations
- Pressure to win trophies immediately
- Expensive contracts
- More prominent and demanding players
- Greater media scrutiny

An elite club was therefore easy in terms of resources but demanding in terms of results.

### Starting with a smaller club

A lower-league or financially weaker club introduced a different set of problems:

- Small transfer and wage budgets
- Limited staff
- Poorer facilities
- Part-time or less talented players
- Difficulty attracting established footballers
- Dependence on free transfers and loans
- Greater financial vulnerability

This could make the interface harder to learn because the player had less room for error. However, smaller-club careers often provided a clearer long-term objective: stabilize the club, improve the squad, win promotion, and gradually build its reputation.

The club-selection screen therefore performed an important piece of implicit onboarding. It allowed experienced players to choose their preferred challenge without forcing everyone through the same scripted opening.

---

## 5. Arrival at the club

After selecting a club, the player was not taken through an animated presentation or a conversation with the chairman. The game placed the manager into the central management interface.

This moment was deliberately overwhelming.

The player suddenly had access to:

- The first-team squad
- Reserve and youth players
- Coaches and other staff
- Club finances
- Fixtures
- Competitions
- Training schedules
- Tactics
- Transfer activity
- Player and staff search
- News and messages
- Board information
- Club records and history

This was the essence of CM 03/04’s onboarding philosophy: **expose the complete system almost immediately**.

The game simulated responsibilities including team selection, tactical planning, training, recruitment, player sales, media interaction, squad morale, and relationships with the board. 

---

## 6. The news screen as an operational guide

The closest thing CM 03/04 had to a guided onboarding system was its **news or inbox screen**.

Instead of giving the player a tutorial checklist, the game presented events as messages. Early messages might concern:

- The manager’s appointment
- Board expectations
- Competition participation
- Preseason fixtures
- Transfer developments
- Injuries
- Staff recommendations
- Contract situations
- Squad registration
- Media questions

These messages gradually introduced the game’s responsibilities in context. An injury message encouraged the player to inspect the squad. A transfer message linked to the relevant player or club. A board message clarified expectations. A match-preview message signaled that the player needed to choose a team and tactics.

This was a form of **event-driven onboarding**.

Rather than saying, “Now open the tactics screen,” the game created a reason for the player to visit it. Rather than explaining contracts abstractly, it informed the manager that a player’s deal was approaching its conclusion.

### The Continue button

The primary rhythm of play revolved around the **Continue** command.

Pressing Continue advanced the simulation until:

- A new message required attention
- A decision arose
- A match approached
- A transfer offer arrived
- An injury occurred
- Another important event interrupted processing

The documentation also listed the space bar as a shortcut for continuing the game or moving through unread news. Other function-key shortcuts provided direct access to the squad, staff, finances, fixtures, transfers, news, competitions, player search, manager information, game status, and training. 


This established the principal gameplay loop:

1. Read the latest messages.
2. Follow links to players, clubs, or competitions.
3. Make decisions.
4. Adjust the squad or tactics.
5. Press Continue.
6. Respond to the next event.

Understanding that loop was the most important onboarding milestone.

---

## 7. Learning to read the squad

The squad screen was normally one of the first places a new manager visited.

Players were represented through rows of information rather than detailed three-dimensional models. Evaluating the team meant understanding:

- Playing positions
- Age
- Fitness and condition
- Morale
- Match form
- Contract status
- Transfer value
- Availability
- Technical, mental, and physical attributes

Attributes were generally expressed on a numerical scale, with higher values representing stronger ability. The difficulty was that newcomers were not immediately told which attributes mattered most for each position.

For example:

- A goalkeeper required goalkeeper-specific qualities rather than conventional outfield technique.
- A central defender needed positioning, marking, tackling, heading, and physical strength.
- A midfielder might require passing, decisions, creativity, teamwork, and stamina.
- A striker could depend on finishing, movement, pace, composure, and heading, depending on the intended role.

The onboarding did not provide a modern role-selection system that highlighted all recommended attributes. The player learned by comparing footballers, reading descriptions, watching performances, and observing match ratings.

This made the squad screen intimidating at first, but it also produced a strong sense of discovery.

---

## 8. Tactics and team selection

Before the first match, the player needed to understand two connected activities:

1. Choosing the starting eleven and substitutes
2. Defining the tactical system

The tactics interface allowed the manager to arrange players on a pitch and configure how the team should play. The exact tactical experience combined formation design, player positioning, and team or individual instructions.

A newcomer had to think about:

- Formation
- Positional balance
- Defensive and attacking responsibilities
- Passing style
- Tempo
- Width
- Pressing
- Marking
- Tackling
- Set pieces
- Individual instructions

The game did not begin with a guided exercise demonstrating how changing one instruction affected behavior. It expected the player to apply their knowledge of football.

This was both a barrier and a strength. Someone who understood football formations could quickly build a recognizable system, while someone unfamiliar with the sport could feel lost among positions, arrows, roles, and instructions.

The 2D match engine then acted as the main source of feedback. Players could watch their tactical choices unfold from a top-down perspective rather than relying solely on textual commentary. ## 9. Training and staff

Training formed another part of the initial learning curve.

The player could inspect training schedules and assign players to regimes intended to improve or maintain different aspects of performance. Staff quality influenced the club’s ability to coach, scout, and support the squad.

A new player needed to discover:

- Which coaches were available
- Whether the coaching workload was balanced
- Which players belonged in each training group
- How demanding schedules affected condition
- Where the club lacked specialist coaches
- Whether additional scouts or physios were required

Again, the game offered tools rather than a guided curriculum. Default arrangements allowed the player to continue without immediately mastering every training mechanism, but more experienced managers could reorganize the entire structure.

This use of acceptable defaults was important. CM 03/04 was complex, but not every system had to be optimized before the first match.

---

## 10. Transfers as practical onboarding

Transfers gave the player one of the clearest goal-oriented learning experiences.

The process generally involved:

1. Identifying a weakness in the squad.
2. Searching for a suitable player.
3. Reviewing attributes, age, value, and contract information.
4. Asking a scout for an assessment when appropriate.
5. Declaring interest or submitting an offer.
6. Negotiating with the selling club.
7. Offering the player a contract.
8. Waiting for the relevant parties to respond.
9. Registering or integrating the footballer into the squad.

The game allowed managers to declare interest before making a formal bid, and responses arrived after time passed in the simulation. citeturn1search11

This system taught several aspects of the interface simultaneously:

- Navigation between clubs and players
- Use of search filters
- Interpretation of player attributes
- Budget management
- Wage negotiation
- News-message responses
- The passage of time

Unlike a tutorial transfer in a modern game, however, the player was not protected from failure. A club could reject the bid, the board could restrict spending, or the footballer could refuse the contract.

---

## 11. The first match

The first match acted as the practical examination at the end of onboarding.

Before kick-off, the manager needed to:

- Select eligible players
- Resolve injuries and suspensions
- Choose substitutes
- Confirm the formation
- Set tactical instructions
- Assign specialist responsibilities where necessary

During the match, the player encountered:

- Text commentary
- The 2D overhead pitch
- Match statistics
- Player ratings
- Tactical adjustments
- Substitutions
- Injuries
- Cards and disciplinary events

The first match converted the database from a collection of numbers into a living system. A defender with strong attributes could still perform badly. A tactical plan might leave space in midfield. A striker might receive no service. A substitute could change the match.

This created the game’s real learning loop:

> **Inspect data → make a hypothesis → configure the team → watch the match → interpret the result → revise the plan**

The onboarding was therefore not truly completed when the player reached the club. It was completed over several fixtures as the player connected attribute data, tactical choices, and match behavior.

---

## 12. What made the onboarding effective

Despite the lack of a formal tutorial, the onboarding had several strengths.

### Immediate agency

The player could take control of almost any available club and immediately make meaningful decisions. There was no long scripted introduction delaying access to the simulation.

### Learning in context

Systems revealed themselves through events. Injuries introduced squad depth, transfer offers introduced negotiation, and fixtures introduced tactics.

### Strong football authenticity

The onboarding made the player feel like a manager because it gave them responsibilities rather than tutorial challenges.

### High replayability

Because onboarding was not scripted, starting a new save remained fast and relatively unobtrusive for experienced players.

### Useful defaults

The player could reach the first match without redesigning every training schedule, replacing every staff member, or mastering every transfer mechanism.

### Interface consistency

Players, staff, teams, competitions, and news items were interconnected through hyperlinks and common navigation patterns. Once the player understood one type of profile, other parts of the database became easier to explore.

---

## 13. What made it difficult

The same design also created substantial barriers.

### The game explained too little

It showed the player a large simulation but did not carefully prioritize what needed attention first.

### Information overload

A new manager encountered dozens of screens, hundreds of players, and many numerical attributes almost immediately.

### Poor explanation of consequences

It was not always obvious how training intensity, tactical instructions, morale, staff quality, or player attributes interacted.

### Dependence on football knowledge

The game assumed familiarity with positions, formations, transfers, contracts, and league structures.

### Hardware decisions appeared before gameplay understanding

New players had to choose active leagues and simulation detail before knowing how those decisions would affect a long career.

### External learning was often necessary

Manuals, printed guides, forums, experimentation, and advice from other players formed an unofficial extension of the onboarding. The continued existence of “newbie queries,” tactical discussions, and player-recommendation threads illustrates how community knowledge supplemented the in-game experience. 

---

## Overall assessment

The onboarding of *Championship Manager 03/04* can best be described as **direct, systemic, and intentionally hands-off**.

It did not teach the player through a formal sequence. Instead, it followed this structure:

1. **Configure the simulation**
2. **Create a managerial identity**
3. **Choose a club and implicit difficulty level**
4. **Enter the full management interface**
5. **Use news messages to identify immediate responsibilities**
6. **Inspect the squad, tactics, training, and finances**
7. **Prepare for the first match**
8. **Learn through results and repeated decisions**

From a modern user-experience perspective, it was harsh. From a simulation perspective, it was remarkably effective. The player was not treated as someone completing a tutorial. They were treated as a newly appointed manager who had inherited an operating football club and needed to understand it quickly.

That abrupt transition was central to the game’s appeal. Confusion gradually became familiarity; familiarity became routine; and routine eventually became the famous “one more match” cycle for which the series was known.
